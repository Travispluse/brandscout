# Write a One-Page Naming Memo Before You Buy the Domain

Most naming mistakes do not happen because the founder is careless. They happen because the decision is made in fragments. A domain search happens in one tab. A trademark search happens later, if at all. Someone checks Instagram on their phone. Someone else says the name sounds cool. Then the team buys the domain because it is available and momentum feels better than uncertainty.

A one-page naming memo fixes that problem. It does not turn naming into a giant branding project. It simply forces the important tradeoffs into one place before money, design work, and public announcements make the decision harder to reverse.

Use this memo when you are choosing a startup name, renaming a local business, launching a new product line, or deciding whether a domain compromise is acceptable. The goal is not to prove that a name is perfect. The goal is to decide whether it is strong enough, clear enough, and safe enough to build on.

## Why a Naming Memo Works

Names are emotional. People react to rhythm, taste, personal associations, and whether the domain is available at that exact moment. That is normal, but it can lead to weak decisions when every conversation starts from scratch.

A memo creates a shared record. It answers the questions that keep coming back:

- What does the name mean?
- Who is it for?
- Which domain will we use?
- What handle compromises are acceptable?
- What risks have we checked?
- What would make us reject this name?

The memo also helps future you. Six months later, when someone asks why you chose `getexample.com` instead of paying for `example.com`, you have the reasoning in writing.

## The One-Page Naming Memo Template

Keep the memo short. One page is the constraint that makes it useful. If you need ten pages to defend the name, the name may be carrying too much explanation.

Use these sections.

## 1. Candidate Name

Start with the exact spelling of the name and the exact casing you expect to use in the logo, website, app, and social profiles.

Example:

`Northline Studio`, written as two words, with no punctuation.

This looks obvious, but it prevents drift. Without a fixed spelling, one person writes NorthLine, another writes Northline, and a third tries North Line. That creates messy domains, inconsistent social bios, and avoidable design revisions.

Also list any rejected spellings. If you considered `Norhtline`, `Northlyn`, or `NorthlineHQ`, note that they were rejected and why. This keeps the team from reopening the same options every week.

## 2. Plain-English Meaning

Write one sentence that explains what the name is meant to signal.

Good examples:

- "Northline suggests direction, reliability, and a clear path for growing service businesses."
- "BrightLedger combines financial clarity with simple bookkeeping."
- "LocalSprout signals practical growth for neighborhood retailers."

Avoid vague claims like "It feels premium" or "It sounds modern." Those reactions may be true, but they are hard to evaluate. A better sentence connects the name to the business strategy.

If the name is invented or abstract, explain the association you want customers to make. If the name is descriptive, explain how it will stay flexible as the business grows.

## 3. Audience and Category Fit

A good name fits the people who need to remember it. Write two or three sentences about the target audience and buying context.

For example:

"This name is for non-technical small business owners who want help launching a simple, trustworthy website. They will likely hear the name in referrals, search for it on Google, and see it in email signatures. Clarity matters more than cleverness."

That paragraph changes the naming standard. A playful name might work for a creator brand but fail for a tax advisory firm. A highly literal name might work for a local repair company but feel limiting for a software platform with a national market.

Judge the name against the audience, not against your personal taste.

## 4. Domain Plan

List the primary domain, backup domains, and the domain tradeoff in plain language.

Include:

- Exact domain you plan to buy
- Whether the exact .com is available, taken, parked, or expensive
- Alternate extensions considered
- Modifier domains considered, such as get, try, use, app, studio, or hq
- Renewal cost if it is a non-standard extension

Then write the decision in one sentence:

"We will launch on `northlinestudio.com` because the exact brand plus category is available, easy to say, and better for customers than using an obscure alternate extension."

This section is especially useful when the .com is taken. A modifier or alternate TLD can be perfectly reasonable, but the team should know why that path was chosen and what future upgrade would be worth considering.

## 5. Social Handle Plan

List the platforms that actually matter. Do not waste the memo checking every network if the business will never use them.

For each platform, note the ideal handle and the acceptable fallback.

Example:

- Instagram: `@northlinestudio`, fallback `@northline.studio`
- LinkedIn: `Northline Studio`
- YouTube: `@northlinestudio`, fallback `@northline_studio`
- TikTok: not a launch priority

The key question is whether the handles still feel like the same brand. A slight punctuation difference is usually fine. Random numbers, long suffixes, and inconsistent words create confusion.

If a handle is taken by an inactive account, note that too. Do not build the whole plan around recovering it unless you have a realistic path.

## 6. Search and Conflict Notes

This is not a replacement for trademark advice, but it catches obvious problems early.

Search the candidate name by itself, the name plus your category, the name plus your city if local, and the domain without punctuation. Check search engines, major social platforms, app stores if relevant, and industry directories.

In the memo, write what you found:

- No similar business in the same category
- Similar name exists in another country but unrelated industry
- Exact phrase has negative news results
- Exact .com belongs to an active competitor
- Marketplace seller already uses the name

Be honest. A naming memo is not a sales pitch. If the search results are messy, write that down. The point is to make the risk visible before you spend money on identity design, signage, packaging, or content.

## 7. Say-It-Out-Loud Test

Write how the name sounds in real use:

- "Thanks for calling Northline Studio."
- "Email us at hello@northlinestudio.com."
- "Search for Northline Studio on Instagram."
- "The invoice will come from Northline Studio."

Then note any spelling problems. Do people hear one word or two? Do they confuse similar sounds? Does the name need explanation every time?

This test is simple, but it is one of the fastest ways to spot a weak domain. If a customer cannot hear it, type it, and repeat it, the name will cost you attention every day.

## 8. Rejection Criteria

Before making the final call, write what would make you reject the name.

Examples:

- A confusingly similar business appears in the same category
- The primary domain renewal cost is too high
- The only available handles require random numbers
- Customers consistently misspell the name in tests
- The name boxes the business into one service we may outgrow

Rejection criteria keep the decision rational. Without them, teams often keep pushing a name forward because they have already spent time on it.

## 9. Final Recommendation

End with a short recommendation. It should be boringly clear.

Example:

"Recommendation: buy `northlinestudio.com`, reserve the matching Instagram and LinkedIn names, and move forward with this brand for launch. The main tradeoff is that the name is more descriptive than distinctive, but that is acceptable because the business depends on local trust and referral clarity."

If the recommendation is not clear, do not buy the domain yet. Do one more round of research or compare it against another candidate.

## A Memo Beats a Naming Debate

A one-page naming memo will not guarantee a famous brand. It will not replace legal review for high-stakes launches. It will not make every stakeholder love the same name.

What it does is more practical: it turns scattered opinions into a decision you can inspect. It helps you see whether the domain, handles, audience, category, and risks support the name. It also gives you the confidence to stop searching when a name is good enough.

That matters because the best name is not the one you debate forever. It is the one that is clear, available, defensible, and ready to carry the business into the market.
