# Build a Search Query Map Before Launch

Search does not wait for a brand to feel ready.

As soon as a founder post, partner email, podcast mention, sales deck, Product Hunt comment, or LinkedIn profile goes live, people start searching in messy ways. They search the exact name. They add the category. They type the modifier from the domain. They search "pricing," "reviews," "login," "support," "alternatives," or the city where they think the business is based.

Most launch teams think about this as keyword research. That is too broad for the first pass.

A search query map is smaller and more operational. It lists the searches real people are likely to try during launch week, then assigns each query to the official page, profile, or answer that should handle it. It is not a full SEO strategy. It is the bridge between a clean name, a crawlable site, and a public brand story that does not fragment the moment people start looking.

If you have not checked how the name behaves in public search yet, start with the [branded search dry run](/blog/branded-search-dry-run-before-launch). If your launch site already has several pages, pair this with the [internal link map for a new brand site](/blog/internal-link-map-new-brand-site). The query map sits between them: it decides what each search should find.

## Start With The Searches That Include The Brand

Do not begin with every keyword the business might someday rank for.

Begin with the queries someone will type after hearing the new name once:

| Query type | Example | What the official answer should do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exact brand | `Northline` | Make the official brand easy to identify |
| Brand plus category | `Northline scheduling software` | Connect the name to the right market |
| Brand plus domain modifier | `getnorthline` | Reinforce the domain and handle pattern |
| Brand plus pricing | `Northline pricing` | Route buyers to plans, sales, or waitlist copy |
| Brand plus support | `Northline support` | Point to a monitored help or contact path |
| Brand plus reviews | `Northline reviews` | Avoid leaving trust questions unanswered |
| Brand plus login | `Northline login` | Clarify whether a login exists yet |
| Common misspelling | `North Line scheduling` | Decide whether redirects, copy, or profiles can catch it |

These are not vanity searches. They are customer behavior.

Someone who searches the name plus "pricing" may be closer to buying than someone who lands on the homepage. Someone who searches the name plus "support" may already be stuck. Someone who searches the name plus "reviews" may be checking whether the brand is real.

The query map makes the team answer a practical question: when that search happens, what should the person find first?

## Add Category And Problem Searches You Can Honestly Own

After branded queries, add a small set of category and problem searches.

The key word is small.

New brands often create a giant keyword list, then treat it like a content calendar. That creates vague launch pages, thin blog posts, and title tags that sound like everyone else. For launch, you need the searches that explain the brand's lane.

Use three buckets:

| Bucket | Example | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Category | `scheduling software for home service teams` | Helps strangers understand what kind of thing the brand is |
| Problem | `assign jobs to field crews without spreadsheets` | Captures the pain the homepage should explain |
| Decision | `Northline vs Jobber` or `home service scheduling alternatives` | Prepares the team for comparison intent |

Do not add a query unless the brand has a page that can answer it honestly.

If the product is still invite-only, do not optimize around a fully open "best software" promise. If the brand serves one narrow audience, do not chase broad category terms that make the offer sound larger than it is. If the comparison page does not exist yet, write down the query and mark the answer as "not ready" instead of pretending the homepage covers it.

This is where the [category language sheet](/blog/category-language-sheet-before-launch) matters. The map should use the same category words the rest of the launch uses. If the homepage says "field service scheduling software" and the LinkedIn page says "AI operations platform," the query map will expose the mismatch quickly.

## Assign Every Query To One Primary Answer

A query map is useful only when it chooses.

For each query, pick one primary answer. That answer might be a page, a social profile, a help article, a marketplace listing, or a short note in the launch record.

| Query | Primary answer | Secondary support | Owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `Northline` | Homepage | LinkedIn company page | Marketing |
| `Northline pricing` | Pricing page | FAQ section | Growth |
| `Northline support` | Contact or help page | Support email sender pattern | Support |
| `Northline scheduling software` | Product page | Homepage title and category copy | Product marketing |
| `Northline reviews` | Customer proof page when ready | Review collection plan | Founder |
| `getnorthline` | Homepage on canonical domain | Social handles using the same pattern | Ops |
| `Northline login` | Login page or waitlist explanation | Signup confirmation email | Product |

The primary answer should be the place you want people to trust first.

This prevents a common launch problem: every page tries to answer every search. The homepage mentions pricing, support, product features, founder story, comparison claims, integrations, category language, and launch news. The pricing page repeats the category story. The help page tries to sell. The blog post tries to convert.

Some overlap is normal. But each important query should have one obvious home.

If the answer does not exist yet, the map should say so. "No official answer yet" is useful. It shows a real launch gap. "Homepage, probably" is usually a way to avoid the decision.

## Watch For Query-To-Page Mismatch

The most expensive search mistakes are not always technical.

They are expectation mistakes.

| Query | Weak result | Better result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brand plus pricing | A generic homepage with no plan information | Pricing page, sales route, or "pricing opens after beta" note |
| Brand plus support | Founder LinkedIn profile | Help page, support email, or monitored contact route |
| Brand plus login | Marketing homepage with no login path | Login page, waitlist status, or account access explanation |
| Brand plus reviews | Empty search results | Proof page, testimonial policy, or review plan when available |
| Brand plus category | A vague title tag | Product page with plain category language |
| Brand plus city | Irrelevant directory result | Local landing page or Google Business Profile if location matters |

This is not about tricking search engines into showing a page before it has earned visibility. It is about making sure the official site is prepared for the intent once it gets crawled, shared, and clicked.

For example, if a beta product does not have public pricing, the answer can be honest: "Plans are being finalized. Join the waitlist or book a demo." That is better than letting people search `brand pricing`, find nothing, and assume the business is hiding basic information.

The same applies to support. If customers may need help during launch week, the query map should point to a real contact route. The [brand contact route map](/blog/brand-contact-route-map-before-launch) can handle the deeper operational pass. The query map simply asks whether searchers will find a trustworthy path.

## Include Domains, Handles, And Modified Names

BrandScout users already know the name rarely travels alone.

The exact domain may be taken. The clean social handle may be unavailable on one platform. The public URL may use a modifier like `get`, `try`, `use`, or `app`. The query map needs to include those variants because customers will search them.

Add rows for:

- The exact brand name.
- The primary domain without punctuation.
- The modifier domain, such as `getbrand`.
- The main handle pattern, such as `@getbrand`.
- Spaced and unspaced versions.
- Common plural or singular variants.
- Old names that might still appear in decks, screenshots, or emails.

Then connect those rows to real surfaces.

If the official domain is `getnorthline.com`, make sure `getnorthline` appears consistently enough on the website, social profiles, and launch links that search engines and humans can connect it. If the handle is `@northlinehq`, record why and decide where that pattern must be explained.

This is a practical extension of the [social handle audit before launch](/blog/social-handle-audit-before-launch). The audit checks what you can claim. The query map checks what people may search after seeing what you claimed.

## Use The Map To Fix Titles, Links, And Source Pages

Once the queries are assigned, use the map to improve the site.

Start with the surfaces you control:

- Homepage title and meta description.
- Product or service page title.
- Pricing, demo, waitlist, and signup pages.
- Help, support, FAQ, and contact pages.
- About page and founder bio.
- LinkedIn company page and priority social bios.
- Launch announcement links.
- Press room or partner fact sheet.

For each surface, ask whether it answers the query it is supposed to own.

If the product page owns the category query, its title should not be a vague slogan. If the pricing page owns pricing intent, it should not hide the next step below three sections of positioning copy. If the help page owns support intent, it should use the current brand name, canonical URL, and monitored contact route.

This also guides internal links. A launch article that mentions availability should link to the official name-checking workflow or product route. A homepage section that mentions support should link to the help page, not leave people searching. A category page should link back to the clearest product explanation.

The [launch link ledger](/blog/launch-link-ledger-before-announcement-day) controls the exact URLs you distribute. The query map controls the searches those URLs should make easier to satisfy.

## Keep The First Version Small

A launch query map should fit on one page.

That constraint is useful. It forces the team to focus on searches that can affect trust, conversion, or confusion during the first few weeks.

Start with 20 to 40 rows:

- 8 to 12 branded queries.
- 5 to 10 category or problem queries.
- 3 to 5 comparison or alternative queries.
- 3 to 5 support, pricing, login, or review queries.
- A few misspellings, modifier, or handle variants.

For each row, record:

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Query | The search phrase someone might type |
| Intent | Find, compare, buy, get support, verify trust, or understand category |
| Primary answer | The page or profile that should handle it |
| Gap | Missing page, weak title, wrong link, stale name, or no owner |
| Owner | Person responsible before launch |
| Review date | When to check search results or Search Console |

The review date matters. A query map is a launch hypothesis, not a permanent truth.

## Review It After Real Search Data Arrives

After launch, compare the map to actual evidence.

Use Search Console, analytics, site search, sales questions, support tickets, social DMs, and partner feedback. Look for patterns:

- Queries you predicted but nobody uses.
- Queries you missed that appear repeatedly.
- Brand misspellings that are more common than expected.
- Pricing or support searches that land on the wrong page.
- Category phrases that attract the wrong audience.
- Comparison searches that need a better answer.

Then update the map.

Do not rewrite the whole strategy after one odd query. But do not ignore repeated behavior because it does not match the launch plan. Search is one of the earliest ways customers tell you what they think the brand is.

If the first week creates a lot of messy signals, use the [brand signal triage after launch](/blog/brand-signal-triage-after-launch) to separate urgent fixes from normal launch noise. The query map gives that triage a starting list.

## The Point Is Search Readiness, Not Keyword Theater

A search query map is not impressive because it is large.

It is useful when it gives every important launch search a clear official answer.

Before you publish the next announcement, list the searches people are likely to try. Assign each one to a page or profile. Fix the obvious gaps in titles, links, category language, support routes, and handle patterns. Then save the map near the rest of the launch evidence.

Brand names become real through repeated public signals. Domains, social handles, title tags, profile bios, support pages, and launch links all teach people what to search and what to trust.

Map those searches before the traffic arrives, and launch week becomes easier to read.
